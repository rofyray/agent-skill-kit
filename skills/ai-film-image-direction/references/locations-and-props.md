# Locations, Props, Vehicles, Crowds, and Scene Frames

Use this reference for reusable environments, spatial reference kits, objects, mechanisms, vehicles, crowd systems, storyboards, keyframes, and production stills.

## Treat a location as geography

A location reference should make the space usable, not merely beautiful. Record:

- Entrances, exits, thresholds, windows, stairs, corridors, levels, and major obstacles.
- Stable landmarks that can anchor character placement and reverse views.
- Approximate scale and relationships between important areas.
- Permanent architecture, furniture, fixtures, signage, and environmental wear.
- Motivated light sources, direction, time or weather state, and practical fixtures.
- Exterior context and connections to adjacent spaces when story-relevant.

Create a clean establishing or three-quarter plate that reveals depth and relationships. Add reverse, side, close-detail, overhead, or threshold views only when production needs them. Anchor reverse views to the same landmarks and construction rather than asking the model for a vaguely similar room.

Keep a canonical base location free of temporary people, weapons, vehicles, set dressing, or event damage unless those elements are permanent. Create separate occupied, damaged, weather, day, night, or period states.

## Assign location-reference roles

A location reference may control architecture and geography without controlling the new frame's lens, crop, composition, character placement, grade, or time of day. State this explicitly when the model is likely to inherit the source frame wholesale.

For scene generation, combine the canonical geography with the exact scene state and supplied composition. Do not let a dramatic source angle become the only definition of the space.

## Build props as independent assets

For a narrative prop, define:

- Geometry, dimensions or scale relationship, material, finish, color, era, condition, and construction.
- Front, back, side, open, closed, interior, or detail views needed to understand it.
- Text, markings, symbols, wear, damage, and which are exact or provisional.
- How it is held, worn, placed, attached, opened, or operated when relevant.
- Which character, location, or story state it belongs to.

Use a separate reference for hidden versus revealed details and for mechanical states such as folded, extended, armed, broken, or repaired. A close-detail reference may control a mechanism without controlling the whole scene.

Include a scale cue when absolute or relative size is important. Prefer a consistent measurement plus a recognizable comparison object or body relationship rather than an unsupported adjective such as enormous.

## Design vehicles and complex spaces

Treat exterior, cabin, cargo area, cockpit, and other recurring interiors as related but separate assets. Track door placement, seating, windows, controls, sightlines, materials, and exterior-to-interior continuity.

For complex mechanisms, create neutral construction references and state variants before asking for scene images that combine character interaction, motion implication, and dramatic lighting.

## Plan crowds and recurring background characters

Define the crowd's population logic, silhouette distribution, costume range, era, palette, and density without turning every extra into a unique lead. Create separate lead-extra references for people who must remain recognizable or perform specific actions.

Use exact counts only when story or composition requires them. Verify that generation has not duplicated a lead character or collapsed distinct people into repeated clones.

## Create storyboards, keyframes, and production stills

Consume the supplied scene intent or shot decision. Represent one visible state per frame:

- Who and what are present.
- Exact story moment and state before or after an action.
- Spatial placement and gaze when established.
- Required location landmarks and props.
- Composition, shot size, perspective, and light when supplied or intentionally designed here.
- Continuity inherited from adjacent frames.

Do not describe an entire transition as if it can appear in one still. For start and end frames, make the two states individually complete and compatible with the intended motion between them.

Label exploratory boards separately from canonical identity or location assets. A rough storyboard should not replace a high-fidelity source reference merely because it depicts the right event.
