# Visual Development and Asset Planning

Use this reference to create a visual bible, art-direction contract, asset inventory, or reference strategy before or during production.

## Convert story into visual requirements

Read the available story material for recurring people, places, objects, vehicles, institutions, periods, transformations, and state changes. Separate narrative requirements from aesthetic invention.

Record:

- Project format, medium, audience, genre, tone, era, and intended image uses.
- World rules and visible cultural, historical, technological, or physical constraints.
- Character recognition needs and story-driven costume milestones.
- Repeated locations, geography requirements, time or weather states, and transitions.
- Narrative props, mechanisms, vehicles, crowds, graphics, and in-world text.
- Injuries, damage, contamination, aging, transformations, hidden or revealed states, and other continuity changes.
- Locked references, user preferences, open design areas, and rights assumptions that need confirmation.

Prioritize assets by dependency and reuse. Build identity and geography sources before scene images that depend on them. Do not require the entire library before a low-risk exploratory frame when the user is intentionally discovering the look.

## Build the style contract

Translate references and verbal direction into observable decisions:

- Shape language and silhouette logic.
- Human or creature proportions, facial geometry, eyes, brows, mouths, hands, hair, and costume construction.
- Line weight, line texture, edge behavior, and level of cleanup.
- Rendering medium, surface treatment, material response, shading, gradients, highlights, and shadow edges.
- Depth, perspective, background density, and separation of foreground, middle ground, and distance.
- Palette families, saturation hierarchy, value range, accent logic, skin handling, and environmental color.
- Light-source logic, contrast, atmosphere, texture, grain, and finish.
- Image ratio, resolution class, transparency needs, and other technical requirements.

Assign each reference a role. An identity reference should not silently control lighting or composition. A style reference should contribute drawing or rendering language without donating its characters, props, setting, or story.

When references conflict, identify the conflict and choose or propose a hierarchy. Do not average incompatible styles into an undefined mixture.

## Separate source assets from scene treatment

Reusable source assets should maximize legibility and minimize accidental inheritance:

- Neutral or simple background.
- Stable, soft, readable light unless a specific lighting state is the asset.
- Clear silhouette, material, color, and construction.
- No unrelated people, props, text, atmosphere, camera effects, or dramatic grade.
- Enough views and detail for downstream use without overcrowding the sheet.

Scene images may apply the project's cinematic light, atmosphere, composition, weather, motion implication, damage, and narrative state. Do not treat a dramatic scene frame as the sole canonical identity source when a neutral reference can be created.

## Design the palette and look system

Define palette by story function rather than isolated swatches:

- Base colors that establish the world.
- Character, faction, location, or emotional accents.
- Counter-notes that create controlled contrast.
- Physical sources for important colors, such as signage, wardrobe, practical lights, weather, or production design.
- How the palette changes across acts, locations, timelines, or states.

Use proportions only when they help a specific project. Do not impose a universal palette ratio.

## Create the asset inventory

For each planned asset, capture:

- Asset ID, type, project role, and priority.
- Canonical name and version.
- Visible state, view, expression, time, weather, damage, or transformation.
- Required references and the role of each.
- Locked identity, design, geography, style, count, and text properties.
- Open decisions and prohibited inheritance.
- Intended consumers, such as concept approval, image generation, video reference, editing, or continuity review.
- Rights or permission status when likeness, trademarks, protected designs, or private material are involved.

Use names that remain sortable and human-readable. A useful pattern is `project-type-subject-state-view-version`, adapted to the project's existing convention.

## Manage exploration and approval

Distinguish exploratory concepts from canonical assets. Compare meaningful alternatives against the visual contract, story function, recognizability, producibility, and downstream consistency.

Once a choice is approved:

- Mark the canonical version.
- Record what is locked and what may still vary.
- Retain rejected variants only when they help prevent regression or document a decision.
- Update dependent asset requirements before generating large batches.

Do not let a visually impressive outlier silently redefine the project's style or identity system.
