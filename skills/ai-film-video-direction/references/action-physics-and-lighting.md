# Action, Physics, and Lighting

Use this reference to turn desired events into chronological, physically legible motion under one coherent lighting logic.

## Build a causal timeline

Write action in present tense and event order. Use absolute seconds when the tool accepts precise timing and the duration is locked. Otherwise use relative phases such as opening, early, midpoint, late, and final hold.

For each meaningful beat, resolve:

- Starting state and position.
- Trigger.
- Character, object, or environmental action.
- Contact, resistance, or transfer.
- Camera response when any.
- Physical and material behavior.
- Sound event.
- Resulting state that persists into the next beat.

Avoid simultaneous instructions that cannot physically coexist. Leave enough time for weight shift, travel, speech, reaction, and settle.

## Direct mechanisms and transformations

Tiny labels on a reference sheet rarely control motion. Describe operation step by step:

1. What is stable before activation.
2. What initiates the change.
3. Which part moves first and around what joint, track, hinge, seam, or axis.
4. What makes contact, locks, unfolds, separates, deforms, illuminates, or transfers force.
5. What remains visible during intermediate states.
6. What final state is reached and what proof makes it unmistakable.

Use separate state references when the design changes materially. Do not make a mechanism transform through arbitrary melting or morphing unless that is the intended material behavior.

## Specify physics where it matters

Describe the properties the audience can observe:

- Mass, center of gravity, acceleration, deceleration, momentum, and inertia.
- Weight transfer, balance, foot contact, grip, leverage, recoil, and recovery.
- Friction, drag, suspension, flex, compression, and rebound.
- Collision points, force direction, deformation, breakage, debris, and aftermath.
- Cloth weight, hair lag, liquid viscosity, particle scale, smoke drift, and wind response.
- Vehicle traction, body roll, braking, turning radius, and surface interaction.
- Door, furniture, weapon, prop, and body contact with visible ownership and resistance.

Do not add maximal physical detail to a still dialogue shot. Use it where incorrect motion would break credibility, continuity, story information, or scale.

## Prove scale in the image

When scale is important, combine several kinds of evidence:

- A measurement or order of magnitude.
- Fraction of frame or relationship to a known subject.
- Comparison with a familiar object, body, doorway, vehicle, or structure.
- A visible consequence such as stride length, shadow size, displaced debris, camera clearance, sound delay, or environmental reaction.

Numbers alone often fail. Make the scale observable in blocking, lens behavior, motion, and consequence.

## Motivate lighting

Define a coherent source logic rather than listing attractive adjectives:

- Key source, practical, sky, fire, screen, sign, moon, vehicle, or other cause.
- Direction relative to camera, subject, and environment.
- Source size, hardness, color, falloff, and exposure priority.
- Shadow direction and contact.
- What is allowed to silhouette, clip, fall dark, reflect, or glow.
- Environmental contribution such as bounce, haze, rain, dust, smoke, or reflective surfaces.
- Any change in light and the physical event that causes it.

Multiple sources may exist, but their hierarchy must remain understandable. Do not force a universal backlight, flat-light ban, or style phrase that contradicts the scene's actual source.

## Preserve state after events

Track consequences through the shot and into following shots:

- Open or broken doors stay open or broken.
- Dropped or transferred props remain with their new owner or location.
- Wetness, dust, blood, smoke, damage, debris, and footprints persist.
- Moving vehicles, crowds, weather, and practical lights continue consistently.
- Physical exertion, injury, disorientation, and recovery do not reset at a cut.

Record the leaving state in the shot card and continuity log.

## Reduce overload

Split the generation when the model must simultaneously solve several uncertain mechanisms, rapid contacts, precise hands, large environmental effects, dialogue, expressive acting, complicated camera movement, and a lighting transition.

Simplification should preserve the scene's causal spine. Remove decorative motion before removing the trigger, action, contact, result, or continuity state that makes the event readable.
