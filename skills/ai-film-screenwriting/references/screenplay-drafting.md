# Screenplay Drafting

Use this reference when writing screenplay pages, extending an existing draft, or converting an approved outline into scenes.

## Select the output format

Honor the user's requested format or the existing canonical draft. If none is specified:

- Use readable screenplay formatting in conversation.
- Use Fountain for a portable plain-text screenplay file when persistence or import matters.
- Use another document format only when the user requests it and the host can create and verify it.

Do not reformat an existing project merely because another format is available.

## Apply screenplay form

- Use scene headings that identify interior or exterior, location, and time when the screenplay form requires them.
- Write action in present tense with concrete, filmable behavior and selective sensory detail.
- Keep action paragraphs readable at the pace of the scene. Vary density when clarity requires it.
- Introduce characters clearly and maintain consistent cue names.
- Use parentheticals only when the intended reading would otherwise be materially ambiguous.
- Use transitions, inserts, montage, intercutting, voice-over, and on-screen text when they clarify the storytelling, not as decoration.
- Avoid camera and editing instructions unless the story cannot be understood without them or the user requested a production-script style.

In Fountain, preserve valid scene headings, character cues, dialogue, parentheticals, transitions, notes, and section structure. Do not place planning commentary inside the canonical screenplay unless it is intentionally marked as a note.

## Build each scene as a change

Before drafting a scene, know:

- What state arrives from the prior scene.
- Who is pursuing what now.
- What resists, complicates, or redirects that pursuit.
- What is concealed, misunderstood, or newly discovered.
- What decision, reversal, failure, gain, or cost changes the state.
- What pressure or question leaves the scene and enters the next one.

The turn need not be loud. A withheld answer, changed allegiance, recognized pattern, lost opportunity, or altered interpretation can be decisive.

Enter when the dramatic pressure becomes useful and leave after the consequential change is legible. Do not apply "enter late, leave early" so aggressively that orientation, emotional consequence, or genre atmosphere disappears.

## Write behavior and subtext

Translate interior states into choices, attention, avoidance, objects, timing, and language. A screenplay may use restrained interpretation, but it should not read like a novel that explains what performers and viewers cannot perceive.

Let characters pursue conversational objectives. Dialogue can evade, test, charm, attack, bargain, protect, confess, perform, or delay. Preserve room for actors by avoiding continuous emotion labels, adverbs, and line-reading instructions.

Differentiate voices through worldview and strategy:

- What the character notices first.
- What the character names directly or refuses to name.
- Vocabulary, syntax, rhythm, compression, and use of silence.
- Humor, formality, metaphor, status behavior, and tolerance for vulnerability.

Avoid phonetic caricature. Use light dialect markers only when the user wants them and readability remains high.

## Control exposition

Give information to characters who have a reason to reveal, conceal, distort, or discover it. Prefer conflict, consequence, and concrete evidence over speeches written solely for the audience.

Track information ownership across scenes. Do not let a character act on a fact they have not learned, or repeat information to someone who already knows it, unless the repetition has a new tactic or emotional purpose.

## Draft a feature in coherent units

Long-form generation is an accumulation problem. Before writing each unit:

1. Read the relevant story-bible entries and approved outline steps.
2. Read enough preceding screenplay pages to recover voice, pace, and unresolved action.
3. Check the continuity ledger and locked lines or events.
4. Draft a coherent sequence or connected scene run.
5. Verify cause and effect, character knowledge, setups, props, time, and relationship state.
6. Update the outline state, continuity ledger, and revision log outside the canonical pages.

Do not restart the film's voice or reintroduce established facts at every chunk boundary. Do not jump ahead merely to fit an arbitrary response length. State the exact scenes or page range completed.

## Protect accepted material

When continuing or revising an existing draft:

- Preserve locked scenes, lines, facts, formatting, and character choices.
- Match the established level of description and dialogue density unless changing it is part of the request.
- Do not silently resolve open questions that the user intentionally left undecided.
- Flag a contradiction before choosing a fix that would alter the story's direction.

## Draft verification

Check the pages themselves:

- The requested outline movement is dramatized rather than summarized.
- Scene headings, cues, action, and dialogue are readable and consistent.
- Every participant has the correct knowledge and relationship state.
- Dialogue voices remain distinct under pressure.
- Action is physically and temporally possible at screenplay resolution.
- The scene changes story state and passes useful pressure forward.
- No planning notes, unsupported citations, model instructions, or prompt syntax leaked into the screenplay.
