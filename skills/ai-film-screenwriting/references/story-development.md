# Feature Story Development

Use this reference for premise development, story bibles, character architecture, treatments, outlines, and scene planning. Apply only the layers needed for the user's requested artifact.

## Establish the story contract

Record what the film is promising before expanding it:

- Core premise and dramatic irony, if any.
- Genre, tone, audience, format, and intended experience.
- Protagonist or ensemble center.
- External pursuit, internal pressure, opposition, stakes, and urgency.
- Thematic question or contradiction, without reducing the film to a lesson.
- World rules and factual constraints.
- Ending, transformation, or unresolved destination when known.
- Locked choices, open questions, and adaptation or likeness assumptions that may require rights review.

If the user supplies only a seed, generate a small set of meaningfully different directions and compare their story engines before expanding one. Do not create superficial variants that change names or settings while preserving the same dramatic design.

## Stress-test the feature engine

A premise can sustain a feature when it continues generating difficult choices, escalating consequences, changing relationships, and fresh forms of pressure. Test:

- Agency: the central characters make consequential choices rather than merely receive events.
- Opposition: resistance can adapt, intensify, and expose different weaknesses.
- Escalation: later problems are not just louder repetitions of earlier problems.
- Causality: major turns emerge from prior choices, discoveries, or credible collisions.
- Scope: the concept contains enough transformation for the requested length without padding.
- Genre renewal: the film repeatedly fulfills and complicates its central promise.
- Ending pressure: the climax forces the core conflict or contradiction into an irreversible choice.

When the engine is weak, repair the premise, opposition, stakes, relationships, or goal before adding scenes.

## Build the story bible

Use the following fields when relevant:

### Project identity

- Working title and version.
- Logline and short premise.
- Genre, tone, audience, rating, language, and approximate scope.
- Thematic tensions and recurring dramatic questions.
- Narrative mode, chronology, point of view, and any formal rules.

### World

- Time, place, institutions, social pressures, and physical rules.
- What is ordinary, forbidden, scarce, dangerous, or changing.
- Locations that carry story functions, not merely visual appeal.
- Research facts that are verified, uncertain, intentionally altered, or invented.

### Characters

For each principal character, track:

- Public identity and role in the story system.
- External want, immediate needs, private pressure, fear, contradiction, and capacity for action.
- What the character believes at the beginning and what experience may challenge it.
- Resources, limitations, secrets, obligations, and moral thresholds.
- Primary relationships, status dynamics, dependencies, and unresolved history.
- Arc state at major story turns, including the possibility of a flat, failed, circular, or ambiguous arc.
- Voice principles: attention, vocabulary, sentence shape, humor, avoidance, and what remains unsaid.

Do not force a trauma, misbelief, redemption, or transformation when the story does not need one.

### Story promises and continuity

- Setups, questions, motifs, objects, rules, and promises that require later handling.
- Information ownership: who knows, suspects, misunderstands, or conceals each consequential fact.
- Timeline anchors, travel constraints, injuries, resources, and relationship changes.

## Choose the right outline depth

Use the lightest artifact that supports the next decision:

1. Logline: protagonist, disruptive situation, pursuit, opposition, and stakes.
2. One-page synopsis: the complete causal arc, including the ending.
3. Treatment: the film as a readable dramatic narrative with major turns and emotional progression.
4. Sequence map: a small number of sustained dramatic movements, each with a local drive and irreversible outcome.
5. Scene list: ordered scene purposes, turns, and consequences.
6. Step outline: scene-by-scene dramatic action detailed enough to draft pages reliably.

These are levels of resolution, not mandatory gates. A nonlinear, episodic, ensemble, mystery, or experimental film may organize its logic differently. Make the causal and informational design legible without flattening the chosen form.

## Use scene cards

For each planned scene, record only fields that affect the story:

- Scene identifier and provisional heading.
- Time, place, participating characters, and point of dramatic attention.
- State entering the scene: knowledge, relationship, pressure, and unresolved action.
- Immediate pursuit and resistance.
- Tactic or approach, when useful.
- Turn, revelation, reversal, decision, or failure.
- State leaving the scene and the pressure passed forward.
- Setup, payoff, planted object, continuity requirement, or deliberate ambiguity.
- Locked line, action, location, or production condition supplied by the user.

A scene may be quiet, observational, or atmospheric, but it should still alter understanding, expectation, relationship, opportunity, or pressure. Combine or remove scenes that perform the same dramatic work unless repetition is itself the design.

## Evaluate the outline

Before drafting, test the whole rather than scoring isolated beats:

- Can each major movement be traced to prior causes?
- Does the protagonist or ensemble meaningfully affect the outcome?
- Are crucial turns prepared without becoming predictable?
- Does information arrive at the most dramatically useful time?
- Do subplots pressure, mirror, contradict, or complicate the central movement?
- Do relationships evolve through action rather than only explanation?
- Are genre and tonal promises renewed across the film?
- Does the ending resolve or deliberately transform the film's central dramatic question?

Report structural alternatives as choices with tradeoffs. Do not present a preferred beat sheet or act percentage as a universal correctness test.
