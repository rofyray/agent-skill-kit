# Critique Matrix

Use this guide to critique one artifact, compare options, examine audience alignment, or support the critique stage of a workshop.

## Establish the evidence

Identify:

- The creator's actual reaction and reasons.
- The intended audience and desired response.
- Observed audience feedback, behavior, or outcomes.
- The important choices embodied in the artifact.
- Craft constraints that affect execution.

Keep reported reactions and observed behavior separate. When audience evidence is unavailable, make a prediction only when useful, label it as inferred, and state confidence. Do not invent consensus or treat popularity as objective quality.

## Place reactions in the matrix

| Creator reaction | Audience reaction | Interpretation | Useful inquiry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Likes | Likes | Positive alignment | Which decisions produced the shared response, and are they repeatable? |
| Dislikes | Dislikes | Negative alignment | Which obvious weakness should be removed, and is further study worthwhile? |
| Dislikes | Likes | Audience misalignment | What value is the audience receiving that the creator does not personally value? |
| Likes | Dislikes | Creator misalignment | Is the gap caused by craft, communication, audience choice, timing, or a genuinely unfamiliar direction? |

The matrix is a questioning tool, not a scorecard. Different decisions inside one artifact may belong in different quadrants.

## Analyze decisions rather than vague quality

Break the artifact into consequential decision units appropriate to the medium. Examples include argument, framing, opening, information hierarchy, tone, interaction, visual direction, pacing, or call to action.

For each material decision:

1. Describe the choice without praise or blame.
2. Cite the creator and audience evidence.
3. Identify the likely quadrant.
4. Separate taste from craft.
5. Explain what the discrepancy teaches.
6. Propose a test, revision, or new direction when the evidence supports one.

Taste concerns what should exist and which direction feels right. Craft concerns how well the chosen direction is executed. A strong concept with confusing expression is primarily a craft gap. A polished artifact pursuing the wrong audience response is primarily a taste or framing gap. Some findings involve both.

## Compare options

Compare options on the dimensions established in the creative frame. Do not force a winner when the evidence supports different tradeoffs. Identify which option is strongest for which audience or purpose, where the creator's preference differs, and which uncertainty a small test could resolve.

When every option occupies the same conceptual neighborhood, say so. Recommend another exploration across a meaningful axis such as premise, audience promise, structure, tone, mechanism, or degree of novelty rather than requesting cosmetic variations.

## Return the critique

Use the lightest structure that keeps evidence clear. Include:

- What is working and should be preserved.
- The highest-impact decision gaps.
- Matrix placement with evidence and confidence.
- Taste and craft distinctions.
- The next decision, test, or exploration.

Do not rewrite or choose for the user unless requested. When the user asks for direct guidance, make a recommendation and explain the audience, evidence, and tradeoff behind it.
